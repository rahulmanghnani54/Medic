 1. Executive summary
 We will build an India-based, medicine-only delivery marketplace. Customers search for a medicine; the
 app finds the nearest partner chemist that has it in stock; a rider delivers within 60 minutes. We hold no
 inventory and run no dark stores. Licensed local chemists fulfil every order, and we earn a commission
 plus a delivery fee.

 Verdict: credible as a focused, compliance-first, single-city play anchored on chronic-care refills, with OTC
 orders driving daily volume. Not viable as a head-on 10-minute speed race against the funded
 quick-commerce players in the metros.

 Market signals worth noting:
  • Quick-commerce platforms have been criticised for delivering prescription drugs, including antibiotics,
    without valid prescriptions. Strict verification is a positioning advantage.
  • The All India Organisation of Chemists and Druggists (AIOCD) asked the home ministry to ban
    10-minute medicine deliveries after Zepto's launch. A chemist-partner model aligns with chemists rather
    than fighting them.
  • In January 2026 the Labour Minister pushed platforms to drop 10-minute delivery claims over rider
    safety. A 60-minute promise is less exposed.
  • State drug regulators have shut quick-commerce stores elsewhere over licensing and storage.
    Compliance must be built in from day one.


 3. Business model: Zomato for medicines
 We are the marketplace layer: chemists list and fulfil, we own the customer, the app and the delivery, and
 we take a commission.

 Where the Zomato analogy holds
  • Asset-light: no inventory or stores; chemists carry the stock.
  • Revenue: commission from chemists, delivery fee from customers, later paid placement for chemists
    and pharma brands.
  • Network effects: more chemists means better stock coverage and faster delivery, which brings more
    users, which attracts more chemists.

 Where it breaks, and what we build instead
  • Customers don't browse chemists. They want a specific drug. The app is "search medicine, we
    match the nearest chemist that has it", closer to Uber than Zomato.
  • Stock accuracy is the core tech. A chemist may not have the strip. Live inventory or instant chemist
    confirmation is essential to the 60-minute promise.
  • Regulation sits inside every order: prescription checks, drug schedules, H1 registers.
  • Lower take rate. Food apps take roughly 20-25%; medicine margins can't support that. We estimate
    8-12%, to be tested with Jaipur chemists.
  • Trust matters more. One wrong or expired drug is a health incident, not a refund.

 scheduled drugs, are released only after a prescription is uploaded and verified by a pharmacist.

 Safeguards to build
  • Tag every catalogue item by drug schedule so the app enforces the prescription rule automatically
    (classification is by schedule, not by dose alone).
  • Limit how many times one prescription can be used and match quantities to the prescription.
  • Detect repeat uploads of the same prescription image, especially for sleeping pills.
  • Show users a clear "pharmacist verifying" step (about 5-10 minutes) so the 60-minute promise stays
    credible.
  • 24/7 pharmacist cover for the verification queue.

  Regulatory caution. We believe India's e-pharmacy rules are still not finalised. Confirm current central rules and
  Rajasthan state drug-licensing requirements with an Indian pharma lawyer before launch. Pricing cannot exceed
  MRP, and many drugs are price-capped under DPCO/NPPA; verify current rules.


 5. Jaipur launch plan
  • Chemist supply first: sign 30-50 partner chemists across dense zones such as Malviya Nagar,
    Vaishali Nagar, Mansarovar, C-Scheme and Raja Park.
  • Stock reliability: require instant stock confirmation from chemists; move to live inventory sync with the
    larger partners.
  • Delivery: start with a hyperlocal delivery partner, then add own riders in the densest zones.
  • Anchor customers: chronic-care patients (diabetes, BP, thyroid, cardiac) on monthly refills, plus OTC
    everyday orders for habit.
  • Legal: Rajasthan drug licence position, chemist partner agreements, pharmacist liability terms.

 What this means
  • Margins are thin. Platform-funded discounts would make most orders loss-making; chemists should
    fund discounts from their own margin.
  • If fixed costs run about Rs 6-8 lakh a month (team, pharmacists, tech, office), break-even needs roughly
    40,000-50,000 orders a month, about 1,500 a day. That is ambitious for one tier-2 city.
  • Levers to improve: chronic-care refills (often Rs 1,500+ order value), batched deliveries, minimum order
    value for free delivery, paid extras such as teleconsults and lab-test bookings, and chemist paid
    placement.
